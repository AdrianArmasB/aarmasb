Adrian Armas Braithwaite

PhD Candidate in Economics

University of Michigan · Department of Economics

I am a fourth-year PhD candidate in Economics at the University of Michigan. My research is in macroeconomics and international finance.

Much of my current work studies how the demographic composition of the population shapes aggregate economic outcomes. In my third-year paper, I show how experience-based expectations sustain deposit dollarization, through belief updating within cohorts and through changes in cohort composition. In my fourth-year paper, with Brian Fujiy and Benjamin Glass, I study the optimal design of transfers when population growth drives long-run growth and fertility has fallen below replacement. In joint work with Marco Ortiz, I study optimal foreign-exchange intervention when banks' portfolio and liquidity frictions generate endogenous deviations from covered and uncovered interest parity.

Before starting my PhD, I worked as a Research Analyst at the Superintendence of Banks, Insurance Companies, and Private Pension Funds (SBS), Peru's financial supervisor. I hold M.Sc. degrees from CEMFI (Madrid) and Universidad del Pacífico (Lima).

Research

I work on macroeconomics and international finance: how households form expectations, how demographics shape aggregate outcomes, and how financial frictions shape exchange-rate policy in emerging economies.

Working Papers

Dollarization Persistence and Expectation Formation In progress

Third-year paper · 2026

Abstract
Deposit dollarization persists after inflation has stabilized. This paper asks to what extent this persistence reflects expectations formed through past experiences. Building on the experience-based learning framework of Malmendier and Nagel (2011, 2016), I argue that heterogeneous lifetime inflation and depreciation experiences across birth cohorts generate differences in beliefs about future depreciation and, therefore, in deposit dollarization. Using three waves of Uruguayan household financial surveys, I document that individuals with higher lifetime depreciation experience hold a larger share of their deposits in foreign currency. I then embed experience-based learning in an overlapping-generations model with incomplete markets and endogenous currency portfolio choice, disciplined by the household-level estimates, and compare its predictions with the aggregate Uruguayan deposit dollarization series. The model highlights cohort replacement as a quantitatively important margin of aggregate de-dollarization, complementing within-cohort belief updating.

Fertility Free-Riding and Optimal Transfers In progress

with Brian C. Fujiy and Benjamin Glass · Fourth-year paper · 2026

Abstract
U.S. fertility has declined for decades, threatening long-run economic growth. This paper identifies a fertility free-riding problem: additional births generate positive externalities for society, but individual families bear all the costs and society does not capture the full social value. We develop an optimal transfer system by embedding a semi-endogenous growth model into an overlapping-generations framework with endogenous fertility, capturing both the intensive margin (number of children) and the extensive margin (share of families with children). Using American Community Survey data, we evaluate whether fertility is inefficiently low and the effects of optimal transfers on U.S. fertility rates and long-run growth.

Presented at: Doctoral Symposium, Hong Kong Baptist University (2026)

Exchange Rate Determination and Optimal FXI Policy: The Role of Portfolio and Liquidity Shocks New version in progress

with Marco Ortiz · Earlier version in the SBS Peru Working Paper Series

Abstract
We develop a model in which banks intermediate capital flows in segmented foreign exchange markets while managing liquidity risk through frictional interbank markets. The interaction of portfolio and liquidity channels generates joint deviations from both uncovered and covered interest rate parity (UIP and CIP). We establish three analytical results: first, settlement frictions create endogenous liquidity premia that drive a wedge between lending, deposit, and reserve rates in each currency; second, the modified UIP condition decomposes into a risk premium—reflecting banks' limited capacity to absorb exchange rate risk—and a liquidity premium—reflecting the relative scarcity of reserves across currencies; third, CIP deviations arise exclusively from liquidity frictions, not from portfolio imbalances. We embed this financial sector in a small open economy general equilibrium model and characterize optimal foreign exchange intervention (FXI) policy. Spot interventions affect both the liquidity and portfolio margins, whereas interventions through non-deliverable forwards operate only through the portfolio channel. The optimal instrument mix depends on the nature of the underlying shock, providing a rationale for the diverse FXI strategies observed across emerging market central banks.

Earlier Work

Default on Debt Payment and Loss of Formal Employment in Peru

SBS Peru Working Paper Series

Abstract
Using individual-level administrative data on income and debt, I quantify the effect of losing formal employment on debt repayment. Losing a formal job increases past-due days by 8.7 days per quarter. The effect is largest among younger and lower-income workers, consistent with these groups having weaker networks to draw on after an income loss.

Liquidity Management in a Bi-Monetary Banking System

CEMFI M.Sc. thesis (advisor: Nezih Guner) · SBS Peru Working Paper Series

Abstract
Liquidity model of a bi-monetary banking system in which banks manage idiosyncratic liquidity shocks through the interbank and FX-swap markets. Banks demand dollars because holding them gives access to an additional source of liquidity, the FX-swap market. U.S. monetary policy affects domestic lending by changing the return on foreign-currency reserves, and an exogenous decline in interbank matching efficiency leads banks to replace domestic loans with foreign-currency reserves.

Liquidity Management in a Totally Dollarized Economy

Universidad del Pacífico M.Sc. thesis (advisor: Saki Bigio) · SBS Peru Working Paper Series

Abstract
Banking liquidity model for a fully dollarized economy without a lender of last resort. The central bank's limited capacity to supply foreign-currency liquidity constrains credit provision and, in the limit, can lead banks to adopt narrow banking.
